Trees Over Roofs, Conservatories and Garages

Tree Over a Roof

Branches resting on or scraping a roof wear tiles, clog gutters and drop leaves and debris. In strong wind, a branch that is only brushing the roof can snap and land on it. We usually lift or reduce the crown to leave a safe gap, and where a tree is too large for its position we'll tell you honestly if removal is the better answer.

Tree Over a Conservatory or Glass Roof

Glass and polycarbonate roofs are the most vulnerable part of a house. A falling branch can break them, and the tree also drops sap, honeydew and debris that stain and clog. We plan these jobs carefully, using sectional dismantling with ropes and rigging, so no piece drops onto the structure.

Leaning Trees

Some trees lean from the day they grew, and many are perfectly stable. A new lean, or a lean combined with soil heaving, cracked ground or exposed roots at the base, can mean the roots are failing. Keep clear and call us. See emergency tree work.

Tree Roots, Subsidence and Heave

A tree surgeon can tell you about the tree: its species, size, condition and how close it stands. We can't tell you why a wall is cracking or a drain is blocked. That takes an engineer, your insurer or a drainage specialist.

Please do not remove a tree you suspect of affecting your building before you have taken advice, and never assume that removing a tree will fix subsidence.

How Trees Can Affect Buildings

On shrinkable clay soils, tree roots draw water from the ground. In a dry summer the soil can shrink, and a building with shallow foundations can move as the ground drops beneath it. This is subsidence. If a large tree on clay is removed, the soil slowly takes water back up and swells, which can lift the building. This is heave, and it can continue for several years.

Broadly, the north of the borough (Norbury, Thornton Heath, Upper Norwood and Crystal Palace) sits on clay soil that shrinks in dry weather, while the southern hills (Purley, Kenley, Coulsdon, Sanderstead and Selsdon) sit on chalk. Older homes, such as Victorian and Edwardian terraces, usually have shallower foundations than modern ones.

Signs of Possible Subsidence

What you seeWhat it may mean
Diagonal cracks, often from the corners of doors and windows, wider at the topTypical subsidence pattern
Cracks that open wider in late summer and autumn, and close in winterSeasonal movement linked to the soil drying out
Doors and windows sticking, or gaps opening at the corners of roomsFrame distortion
Cracks between an extension and the main houseDifferential movement
Hairline cracks in plaster or paintOften just normal settlement or drying out, not subsidence
Cracks that appeared suddenly or widen quicklyGet advice quickly

Only a structural engineer can say what's causing cracks. Trees with a high water demand are most often involved, notably oak, poplar, willow and elm, especially when large and close to the building.

What Happens If You Suspect a Tree

  1. Don't remove or hard-prune the tree yet. This can make things worse, and if the tree is protected it may be illegal.
  2. Tell your buildings insurer. Many policies cover subsidence, usually with an excess.
  3. The insurer typically appoints a loss adjuster and engineers, who investigate cracks, foundations, soil and roots, often with monitoring over several months.
  4. A tree report records the tree's species, size, distance and condition and recommends what tree work, if any, is needed. We can provide it. See tree surveys.
  5. If the evidence points to the tree, the insurer's report usually recommends the tree work. For a protected tree, the application to Croydon Council should include the engineer's and arboricultural evidence.
  6. We carry out the agreed work exactly as specified, and clear up.

How Close Is Too Close?

There's no single legal distance between a tree and a house. What matters is the species, size, soil and foundations.

SituationRule of thumb
Existing tree, no damage or signs of movementUsually fine; monitor it and keep it in proportion with pruning
High water-demand tree on clay, within about its mature height of the houseHigher risk; a survey is worth having
Planting a new tree on clayKeep high water-demand species well away, at roughly their mature height or more; smaller trees can go closer
Building or extending near an existing treeFoundations may need to be deeper; get advice from building control and an arboriculturist

If you're choosing a new tree, ask us before you plant. A small ornamental tree in the right place can be a better choice than a forest-sized one that will cause problems in 30 years.

Trees and Extensions, Driveways and Building Work

Planning an extension, garage, driveway or garden building near a tree? Check before you design:

  • Is the tree protected? A TPO or conservation area affects both the tree and the build. Planning permission doesn't automatically override a TPO.
  • How big is the root protection area? Under BS 5837 the radius is roughly 12 times the trunk diameter, measured 1.5m above ground, up to 15m. A 60cm trunk gives about 7.2m, which can reach well into your plot.
  • Foundation depth. Building near trees on clay may need deeper foundations. Your architect and building control will advise.

We can provide a tree report to pass to your architect, builder or the council. See tree surveys.

Trees Near Overhead Cables

Never work near overhead power lines yourself. Trees close to or touching electricity lines need specialist handling and, in London, usually coordination with the network operator, UK Power Networks. For telephone and broadband lines, the line owner should be contacted. If a tree has brought down a line, stay well clear and call 105. Tell us about any cables when you request your quote so we can plan the right method.

Permission and Neighbours

Check Protection First

Croydon Council says a tree preservation order (TPO) stops visually important trees being removed or pruned without formal permission, and that you must give six weeks' written notice before work to a tree in a conservation area. Fines of up to £20,000 can follow for cutting down a protected tree without permission, and up to £2,500 for pruning one. We check both before any work. TPO guide and conservation area guide.

An engineer's recommendation to remove a tree doesn't remove the need for consent if the tree is protected.

Whose Tree Is It?

A tree belongs to the owner of the land where its trunk grows, and a boundary tree may be jointly owned. We can only work on a tree with its owner's consent, and will not enter a neighbour's land without permission. If a neighbour's tree is damaging your property, talk to them first and keep photos. If the tree is on council land or the highway, report it to the council.

Trees Near Buildings: FAQs

Can you remove a tree right next to my house?

Yes, using the right method for the site, such as sectional dismantling where the tree is too close to fell whole.

Can you remove a tree over my conservatory?

Yes. We use controlled sectional dismantling with rigging, so nothing falls on the structure.

How much does it cost to remove a tree close to a house?

Typically £280 – £780 for a small or medium tree and £1,100 – £3,900+ for a large one, depending on size and access. It costs more than open-garden work because of the extra time, rigging and sometimes a crane. You get a fixed written quote after we've assessed the site.

My walls are cracking and there is a tree nearby. What should I do?

Contact your buildings insurer and consider a structural engineer. We can tell you about the tree, but not what is causing the cracks, and you should not remove a tree you suspect of affecting your building before taking advice.

Can a tree cause subsidence?

Yes, on shrinkable clay soils, especially large, water-hungry trees close to buildings with shallow foundations. But cracks have many causes, so an engineer needs to investigate first.

Will removing the tree stop subsidence?

It can, when the evidence points to the tree, but it can also cause heave if done without advice. Always take engineering and insurance advice first.

What is heave?

When a large tree on clay is removed, the soil slowly takes water back up and swells, which can lift the building. It can continue for several years.

Which trees are most likely to cause subsidence?

High water-demand trees such as oak, poplar, willow and elm, particularly when large and close to a building on clay.

How close can a tree be to a house?

There's no fixed legal distance. It depends on the species, size, soil and foundations. On clay, high water-demand trees should be kept at around their mature height away, and smaller trees can go closer.

Can tree roots damage my drains?

Tree roots can be one cause of blocked or damaged drains, usually entering through existing defects. Who is responsible depends on the pipe, so a drainage specialist or your sewerage company can help.

Can I cut tree roots that are lifting my patio?

We advise against cutting large roots yourself, as it can destabilise or kill the tree. Ask us or an arboriculturist for the safest options.

Will my buildings insurance cover tree work?

It may cover some costs if a tree has damaged your property, and subsidence is commonly covered, usually subject to an excess. Check your policy and keep photos and quotes.

Can I take down a tree my engineer says is causing damage?

If the tree is protected or in a conservation area, you still need council consent or notice first. We prepare the application with the evidence.

What if the tree is on my neighbour's land?

We can only work on a tree with its owner's consent. Talk to your neighbour first and keep a record of the problem.

Can you prune a tree over a power line?

No. Never work near overhead cables yourself, and contact the network operator.

Not sure what your tree needs?

Call and talk it through, or send us photos and your postcode.
