Storm-Damaged Tree: What to Do First and Who Is Responsible

A tree or a big limb has come down. You need two answers: what to do in the next hour, and who pays. The short version is that your own tree is your responsibility, a neighbour's tree is theirs, a street tree is the council's, and the storm itself rarely makes anyone to blame. But the details, and what you do in the first day, decide how smoothly a claim goes. This guide covers both, in the order you need them. Tree down and need it made safe?   • Anyone hurt or in danger? Call 999 first. Right now.
  • Power lines involved? Treat them as live and call 105. Safety numbers.
  • Your own tree: you own it and arrange the clearing. Who is responsible.
  • Neighbour's tree on your property: report it in writing. Neighbours.

  • Insurance: usually covers the damage, not the tree removal. Insurance.

What to do right now

  1. People first. If anyone is hurt or in danger, call 999.
  2. Keep clear of the tree and anything it is touching. It may be under tension, unstable or tangled in power lines.
  3. Treat any wires as live. Do not touch or approach a tree that is in contact with them.
  4. Smell gas? Leave the building and call 0800 111 999.
  5. Keep people and pets away from weakened walls, fences and conservatories.
  6. Photograph the scene from a safe distance, with date and time, before anything is moved.
  7. Do not cut up or move a large tree yourself. Trunks and limbs can roll or spring when cut.
  8. Call your insurer before major repairs, unless urgent work is needed to stop further damage.
  9. Get a qualified, insured tree surgeon to make the tree safe.

Who is responsible?

Responsibility follows ownership, but liability for damage depends on fault.

The legal position, in plain English. There is no special tree law. It is negligence and nuisance. An owner must take reasonable care that their trees do not injure people or damage property. What is reasonable depends on the facts: a big tree beside a road, path or house calls for more care than a small one in a field.

  • If the owner knew, or should have known, that a tree was dead, diseased, decaying or leaning, and did nothing, they are more likely to be liable.
  • If a healthy tree is brought down by exceptional weather, the owner is usually not to blame, and each side claims on their own insurance.
  • Written warnings matter. If you told a neighbour about a worrying tree before the storm, that letter is strong evidence.

This is general information, not legal advice. Every case turns on its facts.

Does home insurance cover a fallen tree?

Check your own wording, but typically:

  • Buildings insurance covers storm damage to the house, subject to an excess.
  • Contents insurance covers items inside.
  • Comprehensive motor insurance covers your car. Third-party-only policies do not.
  • Fences, gates, sheds and greenhouses are often covered only for named causes, and some policies exclude them.
  • Clearing the tree is covered by some policies where it has fallen on a building or blocks access. Routine removal is usually not covered.
  • Trees and plants are often not covered, or only up to a limit.

To protect your claim:

  • Report it quickly and keep the claim reference.
  • Check how your policy defines "storm".
  • Keep every receipt, including urgent steps.
  • Get written quotes before major repairs.
  • Do not throw damaged items away until the insurer has seen them.

If the owner of the tree was negligent, their liability cover is meant to pay for repairs, but when the cause was exceptional weather, both sides usually claim on their own policies.

Evidence to keep

  • Photos and video of the tree, the damage and the surroundings, before anything moves.
  • Date, time and weather.
  • Photos from before the storm, such as street view images or family pictures, showing the tree's condition.
  • Names and numbers of witnesses and neighbours.
  • Letters or messages about the tree sent before the storm.
  • Quotes, invoices and receipts.
  • A report from a qualified arboriculturist or tree surgeon. When we attend, we photograph the failure point and give a written note of what we found, which helps an insurer. See tree surveys.

Common situations

A tree on your house, roof or conservatory

Keep out of the damaged part, avoid wires, photograph from a safe place, tell your insurer and arrange safe removal.

A tree on your car, fence, shed or greenhouse

Stay away from a car if wires are touching the tree. Fences and sheds can often be made safe quickly unless they are unstable.

A neighbour's tree in your garden

  • Photograph it and send your neighbour a written note.
  • Tell your insurer.
  • Agree who arranges removal. It is normally the neighbour, because the tree and the timber are theirs.
  • Keep records. If you had warned them earlier, keep that too.
  • If you had earlier concerns, Citizens Advice says to put them in writing, because it shows you raised the risk.

A council or street tree

Report it to Croydon with photos and the location, using the Love Clean Streets app or the council. A claim against the council usually needs evidence it knew, or should have known, of a defect. Do not cut a street tree yourself.

A tree across a road, railway or tram line

Call 999 if anyone is in danger. Report road trees to the council, or TfL on red routes. Never go near railway or tram tracks.

A leaning, split or hanging tree

Keep clear and get it checked quickly. Warning signs after a storm are a new lean, soil heaving at the base, exposed roots, split limbs and branches hung up in the crown.

Why trees can fall days after a storm

Waterlogged ground loosens roots, and a tree weakened in the storm can fail later. If a tree near your house has shifted, cracked or started to lean, treat it as an emergency even if it is still standing. Southern hillside gardens in Purley, Kenley, Coulsdon, Sanderstead and Selsdon, and large old ridge trees around Crystal Palace and Upper Norwood, are exposed. Brittle ash can drop big limbs, as in ash dieback in Croydon.

Is the fallen tree protected?

A storm-damaged tree can still carry a TPO or sit in a conservation area. Croydon says a dead tree needs no formal permission, a dying one does, and for a dangerous one only the urgent necessary work can proceed without consent, with five days' notice. Keep photos and a report, because the council may ask about the tree's condition. See tree preservation orders and conservation area tree work.

Beware of rogue traders after a storm

Storms bring doorstep callers. Warning signs include offers to clear a tree "today", very low prices, pressure to decide at once, cash upfront, no proof of insurance or qualifications, no questions about protection, and "topping". Ask for a written quote and proof of insurance. See how to choose a tree surgeon.

Storm-damaged trees: frequently asked questions

Who is responsible for a tree that falls in a storm?

The owner is responsible for the tree, but a healthy tree brought down by an exceptional storm usually leaves the owner not to blame.

My neighbour's tree fell into my garden. Who pays?

Usually your own insurer pays for your damage. A neighbour is more likely to be liable if they knew the tree was diseased or leaning and did nothing.

Does home insurance cover tree removal?

Some policies cover clearing a tree that has fallen on a building or blocks access, but routine removal usually isn't covered. Check your wording.

A tree fell on my car. What do I do?

Stay away if wires are touching the tree, photograph it, and call your motor insurer. Comprehensive cover typically covers it.

A council tree fell on my property. Can I claim?

Report it with photos. A claim generally needs proof the council knew, or should have known, of a defect.

Can I cut up a fallen tree myself?

Not a large one. Trunks can be under tension and roll or spring.

What if the tree is touching power lines?

Stay well clear and call 105. Call 999 too if the public is at significant risk.

Do I need permission to clear a protected tree that has fallen?

Dead trees need no formal permission, but give five days' notice. For a dangerous tree, do only the urgent work and tell the council.

Should I photograph the damage before moving anything?

Yes, with date and time, from a safe distance.

Can a tree fall days after a storm?

Yes. Waterlogged ground loosens roots, so keep clear of trees that lean or have cracked.

Who pays for a neighbour's tree to be removed?

Normally the neighbour, because the tree and timber are theirs. Your insurer covers your own damage.
