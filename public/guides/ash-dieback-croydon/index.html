Ash Dieback in Croydon: What Owners Need to Know

Ash dieback is a destructive fungal disease first recorded in south-east England in 2012. It makes affected trees weak and brittle, so ash trees near houses, roads and paths are the ones to check first. But you don't have to rush to fell every ash tree. This guide explains how to spot the disease, how to judge the risk, what the rules are, and what to do.

What Is Ash Dieback?

Ash dieback is caused by a fungus that came to the UK from Asia and has spread across the country since it was first recorded in south-east England in 2012. The spores are carried on the wind, so you can't stop it reaching your tree, and there is no practical cure. Most ash trees are likely to be affected over time, but not all at the same speed. Some decline quickly, others slowly, and a small number appear to show some tolerance. That's why the advice is to assess and monitor, not to panic-fell.

In Croydon, ash is recorded in the woods and downs around Selsdon, Coulsdon and Kenley, and it also grows in gardens and along boundaries across the borough.

Signs of Ash Dieback

Symptoms change through the year, and one sign on its own doesn't prove anything. Look for several together.

WhenWhat to look for
Spring and summerLate or sparse leafing, leaves with black or brown patches that wilt and die, shoots that die back
SummerA thin, see-through crown, with dead twigs and branches among the leaves
Autumn and winterDead twigs and branch tips, bare areas in the crown, and tufts of new shoots on the trunk and main branches
On the barkDiamond-shaped dark lesions where branches meet the trunk, with brownish or grey bark under the lesions
At the baseLesions, decay or fungus, often secondary problems that weaken the tree further

Not every thin ash tree has dieback. Drought, root damage, other fungi and old age can look similar. A professional assessment is the only way to be sure.

Is My Ash Tree Dangerous?

An infected tree isn't automatically dangerous. What matters is how far the disease has gone and what's underneath if it fails. The wood of an affected ash can become brittle, so limbs and whole trees can fail with little warning, and the risk rises as the disease advances and as winter weather arrives.

What to Do: Step by Step

  1. Don't climb it, lean ladders on it or work under it. Brittle wood can fail without warning.
  2. Photograph the tree from several angles, with the date. Repeat once a year to track change.
  3. Work out what's within falling distance.
  4. Get a professional assessment. We can assess it and give you a written report. See tree surveys.
  5. Decide: monitor, prune or remove. The right option depends on condition and risk, not just whether the tree is ash.
  6. Check the rules before any work (TPO, conservation area, felling licence, wildlife).
  7. Use a qualified, insured tree surgeon. Ash dieback trees are among the more dangerous to work on.
  8. Plan replacement planting.

Don't fell it just because it's ash. Healthy-looking ash trees, and those with only light symptoms, may last for years, and some may prove tolerant.

The Rules: What Applies to Ash Trees

Felling Licences

The Forestry Act 1967 says that felling growing trees, including diseased and dying ones, requires a felling licence unless an exception applies, and dangerous trees are among the exceptions. The Forestry Commission expects most ash felling in response to dieback, including felling multiple individual trees, to need an approved licence. But this mostly matters for woodland, fields and larger-scale felling. A licence may be required where the trees are not part of a garden. If you plan to fell more than a few trees, check whether a licence is needed.

SituationFelling licence?
Ash tree in a private gardenGenerally not needed, but check the exceptions
Ash in a hedge, field edge or woodland you ownMay be needed, depending on volume and location. Ask the Forestry Commission
A single ash tree that is immediately dangerousAn exception generally applies, but keep evidence
Several ash trees on land that isn't a gardenLikely needed. Apply, or ask the Forestry Commission first

TPOs and Conservation Areas

Croydon Council says a dead tree needs no formal permission, a dying tree does, and for a dangerous tree only the urgent works necessary can be done without consent, with five days' notice asked for. A tree with ash dieback is usually dying, not dead, so it generally needs consent or six weeks' notice first, unless it's dead or presents an immediate risk. Keep photographs and a report. See our TPO guide, conservation area guide and Do I need permission to fell a tree?

Wildlife

Older ash trees with holes and dead wood are valuable for nesting birds, bats and insects. Where a tree isn't immediately dangerous, plan felling for the autumn and winter, and check for nests and bats first. See our nesting season guide.

Leaves, Timber and Firewood

The fungus spreads from fallen leaves, so clearing and collecting fallen ash foliage helps. Timber and firewood are considered low risk for spreading the disease, so ash logs can generally be kept and burned.

Replanting After Ash Dieback

Don't plant ash. Choose a species suited to the site, such as field maple, hornbeam, rowan, wild cherry, silver birch, hazel or a small ornamental tree, depending on space, and plant away from buildings. A mix of species is more resilient. Where a protected tree is removed with consent, the council may require a replacement.

What Does Ash Dieback Work Cost?

Ash dieback trees can cost more to work on than healthy trees of the same size, because brittle wood means more care, sometimes a platform or crane, and extra safety measures. See our cost guide. Typical ranges: assessment from £260; remove a small or medium ash £300 – £800, including the extra for brittle wood; remove a large, brittle ash near buildings or a road £1,150 – £4,100+.

Ash Dieback FAQs

What is ash dieback?

A fungal disease spread by airborne spores that attacks ash trees, causing leaf loss and crown dieback, and often leading to death.

How do I know if my ash tree has dieback?

Look for sparse leaves, black or wilting leaves, dead twigs and branches, new shoots on the trunk, and diamond-shaped dark lesions on the bark. A professional assessment is the only reliable way to be sure.

My ash tree looks thin. Does it have dieback?

Possibly, but other things can cause thin crowns too, such as drought or root damage. Get it checked.

Do I have to cut down my ash tree?

Not necessarily. You don't need to remove ash trees that aren't showing signs of infection or pose no danger. Monitor them, and act where there's a risk to people or property.

Is an ash tree with dieback dangerous?

It can be, as the wood can become brittle and limbs can fail. The risk depends on how far the disease has gone and what's beneath the tree.

Can ash dieback be cured?

No. You can't stop it reaching a tree, and there's no practical cure. The aim is to manage the risk.

Do I need a felling licence for an ash tree?

Trees in gardens are generally exempt, but felling ash elsewhere, including hedges, fields and woodland, may need a licence from the Forestry Commission. Ask us or the Forestry Commission.

Do I need permission to remove an ash tree with dieback?

Only if the tree has a TPO or is in a conservation area. A tree with dieback is usually dying, not dead, so consent or notice is generally needed.

Can I remove an ash tree in nesting season?

Only after checking for nests and bats, and ideally not unless it's urgent.

Can I burn ash firewood from an infected tree?

Yes. The risk of spreading the disease through timber and firewood is considered low.

My neighbour's ash tree looks diseased. What should I do?

Tell them in writing with photos, and keep a record. If it's an immediate danger, call the emergency services.

A council or street ash tree looks diseased. Who do I tell?

Report it to Croydon Council with photos and the exact location. Don't prune it yourself.

Should I replace an ash tree with another ash?

No. Choose a different species suited to the site.
