Tree Surgeon in Purley, CR8

Purley has some of the most closely managed tree and hedge land in Croydon. William Webb laid out his garden-suburb estates here from the 1890s, planning the landscape before the houses, and two of them, the Webb Estate and Upper Woodcote Village, are conservation areas. Houses stand behind live hedges and mature trees, many roads are private, and some plots carry covenants that go beyond planning law. The two Webb conservation areas

Croydon lists the Webb Estate and Upper Woodcote Village among its conservation areas. The council's draft management plan, published on 16 September 2025, explains their origin:

  • William Webb (1862 to 1930), a chartered surveyor and estate agent, bought the land in 1888 and began building in 1898. Upper Woodcote Village was laid out in 1903, and by 1925 the initial development was largely complete.
  • His 1919 book Garden First in Land Development set out the idea: landscape the site before building, so that plain, restrained houses sat among mature hedges and planting.
  • Purley sits between two valleys cut into the chalk of the North Downs, from about 200 ft above sea level on the valley floor to about 400 ft at the highest point.

Where the plan stands. Cabinet approved the draft, and it becomes a Supplementary Planning Document only if Full Council approves it. The council says it introduces no new planning controls. Brighton Road in Purley is separately one of nine places the council is considering for conservation area status. Check the council's site for the latest, and see conservation area tree work.

What the council expects of your trees

The draft plan is specific:

  • Numerous Tree Preservation Orders are in place across the two areas.
  • A strong presumption against the loss of trees and other green features that contribute to the area's special character, and the council says it will resist losing good-quality trees that make a positive contribution.
  • Proposals are expected to respect mature trees, hedgerows and open views.
  • Owners must know which designations apply to their property and hold the right permissions before work starts. Unauthorised works can lead to enforcement action.

What that means in practice:

  • Pruning is straightforward when it follows good practice. The usual route is written notice six weeks before work in a conservation area.
  • Removal is looked at harder. Good evidence, such as disease, a structural fault or damage to a building, makes the case, and we document it with photographs.
  • Dead or dangerous trees are handled under different rules. See tree preservation orders.

Hedges: the Purley speciality

Webb's plots were separated by mature boundary hedges. The covenants and the council's plan treat hedges as part of the design, which most areas do not.

  • Covenants. The draft plan says Webb's covenants banned walls and fences other than wire fences and live hedges, and required owners to maintain the hedges on their land. It adds that covenants may vary from plot to plot, so check your title documents. We cannot advise on covenants, but we can tell you what the hedge needs.
  • Front hedges. The plan says a front hedge should remain a double-staggered privet hedge with a post and wire at its centre.
  • Side hedges. A mixed hedge of native and climate-tolerant species is acceptable along garden boundaries.
  • Cutting. We keep to the original form, avoid hard cuts into bare wood where the plant will not regrow, and check for nesting birds from March to August. See hedge cutting and nesting season guidance.
  • Trees in hedges. A tree inside a hedge can still be protected.

Private roads, long frontages and access

  • Access. Many roads here are private, so we agree access with you and, where needed, the estate before the day.
  • Building lines. The covenants set houses back from the road, so there is usually a long front drive and good working room.
  • Big trees over big houses. Reduction and sectional removal of mature trees close to buildings is common. See tree pruning, tree removal and trees near buildings.
  • Chalk soil. The RHS says subsidence is generally a problem only on shrinkable clay and that chalk gardens are very unlikely to have tree-related damage. That is a general rule, so check your own ground.
  • Stumps and surveys. See stump grinding and tree surveys.

Parking is usually on the drive. If the road needs a bay suspension, the council wants about two weeks' notice.

Tree Surgeon in Purley, CR8: frequently asked questions

Is Purley a conservation area?

Parts are. The Webb Estate and Upper Woodcote Village are conservation areas, and Brighton Road is being considered. Check your road with the council.

Do I need permission to prune a tree on the Webb Estate?

In a conservation area you give the council written notice six weeks beforehand. There is no fee, and we prepare it.

Will the council let me remove a tree?

It resists the loss of good-quality trees that contribute to the area. Evidence of disease, structural faults or building damage helps, and we gather it.

Can I take out my front hedge?

Check your covenants and the council's expectations first. The draft plan wants front hedges retained as double-staggered privet.

Do hedges need permission to cut?

Not usually in themselves, but trees within them may be protected, and nesting birds are protected from March to August.

My road is private. Can you still get access?

Yes. We agree access with you and the estate beforehand.

Are big trees a risk to a chalk-soil house?

Generally less than on clay, according to the RHS, but check your plot. See our page on trees near buildings.

How much does tree work cost in Purley?

Typically £200 to £1,100 for most jobs, with large trees from £1,000 to over £3,000.

Do you cover CR8?

Yes. Purley is part of our Croydon patch.

What if a tree comes down in a storm?

Phone us in working hours, and use the safety numbers on our emergency page at other times.
