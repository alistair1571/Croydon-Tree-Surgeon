## When do tree roots actually damage a house?

Roots rarely push through sound foundations. The real mechanism is water. On shrinkable clay, a thirsty tree dries the soil, the clay shrinks, and the ground under a shallow foundation drops. The RHS says subsidence is generally a problem only on shrinkable clay, and that gardens on chalk or sand are very unlikely to see tree-related damage.

**Croydon is split.** The north of the borough sits largely on clay and the southern hills on chalk, so your postcode matters. Check your own ground with the British Geological Survey map or your surveyor, because the boundary is not a straight line.

**Three things need to line up:**

1. **Shrinkable clay** under the house.
2. **Shallow foundations.** Houses built before the 1950s are most at risk. Those built after about 1980 usually have deeper footings.
3. **A thirsty tree close by.**

Oak is the species most often involved in UK subsidence cases, according to the RHS. Poplar, willow, eucalyptus and leylandii-type conifers are also high water users. Magnolia, ginkgo and pine are low users.

**Signs worth acting on** (from the Woodland Trust): diagonal cracks wider than about 3 mm, wider at the top, visible inside and out; doors and windows that stick; wallpaper creasing at the ceiling; and cracks where an extension meets the house. Movement is seasonal and tends to peak after leaf fall in autumn. We can't diagnose cracks. See [what a tree survey can and can't tell you](/tree-surveys-croydon/).


## Why removing the tree can make things worse

Felling a big tree on clay can let the soil re-absorb water and swell back up, which pushes foundations upward. This is called heave. The RHS describes it as rare, but a risk when trees are removed on London clay, and says underpinning may then be the only effective cure.

So, if you suspect subsidence:

1. **Do not fell the tree first.**
2. **Tell your buildings insurer.** They will usually send an engineer and run monitoring.
3. **Expect monitoring over time.** Councils and insurers may ask for about 12 months of data showing a seasonal pattern, and precise level readings are more reliable than crack gauges.
4. **Let the evidence decide.** One surveyors' source says 50% to 70% of subsidence claims are turned down, mostly because the damage has a different cause.
5. **Check protection first.** A TPO does not stop felling a tree that is damaging a building, but it still needs consent.


## How close is too close?

There is no legal distance, and the Arboricultural Association says there are no fixed minimum distances for planting. The RHS gives a useful rule of thumb for the reach of roots:

| Tree water demand | Soil drying likely to reach |
|---|---|
| High (oak, poplar, willow, leylandii-type conifers) | About one tree height |
| Medium or low | Unlikely beyond half the tree height |
| Surface lifting of paving | Mostly within 1 m of the trunk |

Roots themselves can spread to up to three times the tree's height, but spread is not the same as harm.

**Planting a new tree?** Pick the species for its mature size, not its size in the pot. Avoid paving or tarmac within 1 m of the trunk. Root barriers are generally not practical, because roots grow around them.


## Roots and drains

Roots find old drains because they leak, not because they are strong enough to break good pipes. The RHS says sound drains generally need no action. If roots have got in through a defect, the options are a resin-lined repair, replacement with plastic pipe, and only as a last resort removing the tree.

- A **CCTV drain survey** shows what is wrong.
- **Responsibility** depends on whose pipe it is. Pipes serving only your house are yours. Shared pipes may be the water company's. Check before you pay.
- **Do not chop large roots yourself.** It can destabilise the tree and hurt it. Ask us first.

## Protected trees and permission

Check for a TPO or conservation area first, even if the tree is in your own garden. Croydon Council says consent is needed to prune or fell a protected tree, there is no application fee, and fines run to up to £20,000 for felling and up to £2,500 for pruning. In a conservation area, six weeks' written notice is needed. An engineer's report recommending removal does not replace consent. See [tree preservation orders](/guides/tree-preservation-orders-croydon/) and [conservation area tree work](/guides/conservation-area-tree-work-croydon/).


## It's the neighbour's tree

You can usually cut branches that overhang your land back to the boundary, but not beyond it, and the wood remains theirs. If you think their tree is damaging your house, tell your own insurer, write to the neighbour with photos, and keep copies. A written warning matters if the tree later causes damage. See [who is responsible for a storm-damaged tree](/guides/storm-damaged-tree-who-is-responsible/) and our homepage notes on [whose tree it is](/#whose-tree-is-it-neighbours-council-and-landlords).


## Cables and safety

If the tree is near overhead lines, do not touch it or work on it. Ring UK Power Networks on 105 for a damaged line. We keep clear of lines and tell you when the network operator has to be involved.

:::faq Trees near buildings Croydon: frequently asked questions
### How close can a tree be to a house?
There is no fixed legal distance. On clay, a high water-demand tree such as oak or poplar can dry the soil out to about its height, and other trees to about half that. On chalk or sand the risk is very low.

### Can tree roots damage house foundations?
Rarely directly. The usual problem is clay shrinking when a thirsty tree takes water, which moves shallow foundations. Older houses on clay are most at risk.

### I have cracks in my wall. Is it the tree?
Maybe. Diagonal cracks wider than about 3 mm, sticking doors and cracks at an extension are worth reporting. Tell your insurer before touching the tree, because other causes such as leaking drains are common.

### Should I cut down a tree causing subsidence?
Not before your insurer and an engineer have assessed it. Removal on clay can cause heave, which can be worse than the original problem.

### What is heave?
Upward movement of foundations when clay swells after a tree is removed. It is rare but can follow felling on London clay.

### Can you prune a tree to stop subsidence?
Sometimes, as part of an engineer's plan. Pruning has to cut water use a lot to work, and fast-growing species regrow within a couple of seasons.

### Can roots block or break my drains?
Roots usually enter drains that are already cracked or leaking. A CCTV survey shows the cause, and a liner or pipe repair often solves it without losing the tree.

### Do I need permission to remove a tree near my house?
If it is protected or in a conservation area, yes. A TPO does not stop you removing a tree that is damaging a building, but consent is still needed.

### Will my insurer pay for tree removal?
Sometimes, where the tree is part of a valid subsidence claim. Ask before booking anyone.

### How much does it cost to take down a tree beside a house?
From about £280 for a small or medium tree to over £3,900 for a large one over a house or conservatory.

### Is a neighbour's tree my problem?
You can cut overhanging branches back to the boundary. If it is causing damage, tell your insurer and write to the neighbour.

### What if the tree is leaning?
A new lean, lifting soil or exposed roots can mean root failure. Keep people away and ring us.
