## What the law says

**Wild birds.** Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 it is an offence to intentionally take, damage or destroy a wild bird's nest while it is in use or being built, to take or destroy eggs, or to kill or injure a bird. Some rarer species, such as barn owls, are also protected from disturbance when nesting. A nest is protected from the moment building starts until the young have left it.

**Bats.** All bats and their roosts are protected. A roost is protected even when no bats are in it, and disturbing or harming bats is an offence at any time of year.

**Other wildlife.** Badgers are protected, and the Flintshire contractors' guidance says work within 30 m of a sett could be illegal without a licence. Stumps and rotting wood can also shelter stag beetle larvae, so we check before grinding.

**Consent does not override wildlife law.** A TPO consent, a conservation area notice or planning permission does not give you permission to harm nesting birds or bats. See [tree preservation orders](/guides/tree-preservation-orders-croydon/).

**Garden hedges have no closed season,** but the same bird protection applies.


## When is nesting season?

The law sets no dates. The RSPB says March to September marks the main nesting season, advises cutting hedges between 1 September and 28 February, and adds that some birds start in January or February while others breed into September. Councils commonly use March to the end of August as working guidance, and the end of September for swallows.

What that means month by month in a typical Croydon garden:

| Months | Tree and hedge work |
|---|---|
| **October to January** | The best window for most jobs |
| **February** | Take care, because early nesters start |
| **March to April** | Expect checks and some delays |
| **May to July** | Peak nesting and bat activity. Only work you can fully check |
| **August** | Late broods, so checks still needed |
| **September** | Late nesters finish, then work picks up again |

Weather shifts all of this. A mild February can start the season early.


## How we check before cutting

1. **Choose the time.** Autumn and winter where the job allows.
2. **Watch first.** The RSPB suggests watching the area for a few days. Birds going in and out regularly suggest a nest. Birds that leave at dawn and only return at night are probably roosting.
3. **Inspect from several sides, and at height where needed.** We look for nesting material, birds carrying food, alarm calls, droppings, feathers and visible nests.
4. **Work in stages through dense cover.** Hedges, conifers and ivy are checked section by section, because small birds such as robins and wrens often nest within a few metres of the ground.
5. **Use hand tools where it helps.** The RSPB suggests manual tools so you can stop if you find something.
6. **Stop and leave a buffer** if a nest is active. We move the work elsewhere and come back after fledging.
7. **Say no to what cannot be checked.** If cover is too dense to inspect safely, we recommend waiting.

Tall leylandii and thick ivy are the hardest to check and a favourite nest site. See [hedge cutting](/hedge-cutting-croydon/) for how to cut them without killing them.


## Bats and tree work

Mature trees, old oaks and ivy-covered trunks are what bats like. The Flintshire guidance lists woodpecker holes, cracks and splits, loose bark and thick-stemmed ivy as places to check, with a powerful torch and binoculars. Signs of bats include dark staining or scratches around an entry hole, smoothed surfaces around a cavity, droppings and squeaking at dusk.

- **If we find signs,** we stop and take advice before touching the tree.
- **If a tree has roost features but no signs,** we use reasonable avoidance: we do not cut into cavities, and we lower limbs gently so any bat can leave at dusk.
- **Records.** We document the checks before felling a mature tree.
- **Licences.** Work that affects a roost normally needs specialist advice and sometimes a Natural England licence.

Croydon's downland edges are bat country. The City of London's plan for Kenley Common records five bat species and says careful surveys are needed before tree work there. See [Kenley](/tree-surgeon-kenley/) and [Coulsdon](/tree-surgeon-coulsdon/).


## Where nesting matters most in Croydon

- **The wooded south** (Purley, Kenley, Coulsdon, Sanderstead, Selsdon): woodland and downland edges. Large trees and conifers are hard to check, so autumn and winter work is best. Happy Valley's own plan leaves scrub for nesting birds.
- **Terraced streets** (Addiscombe, Thornton Heath, Norbury): boundary hedges, front hedges and ivy are common nest sites.
- **Upper Norwood and Crystal Palace:** old gardens with veteran trees.
- **Shirley and the heath edge:** big oaks with cavities.


## What to do if you find a nest

- **Before work starts:** do not start. Wait for the young to leave or take advice.
- **During work:** stop immediately, leave a buffer and keep the area quiet.
- **Unsure if it's active?** Assume it is and watch from a distance.
- **Do not move the nest.**
- **A fallen chick or damaged nest:** keep people and pets away and contact a wildlife rescue or the RSPB.
- **A nest in your roof** needs no action, according to the RSPB. Many birds nest in roof spaces without harm.
- **Suspected offence by someone else:** phone the police on 101 and ask for the Wildlife Crime Officer, or 999 if a crime is in progress. Photograph what you can safely. The RSPB's Wildlife Enquiries line is 01767 693690.


## Emergencies and dangerous trees

If a tree is dangerous or has come down in a storm, safety comes first. Make the tree safe, limit the work to what is necessary, check as far as is safe and record what you found. See our [emergency page](/emergency-tree-surgeon-croydon/) and [storm-damaged trees](/guides/storm-damaged-tree-who-is-responsible/).


## How nesting season affects your job

- **Delays** can last several weeks if a nest is found.
- **Part of a job** may be done and the rest left until later.
- **Return visits** can add cost, and the quote will say so.
- **Large or old trees** may need an ecologist's report at extra cost.
- **Autumn and winter** are the easiest times to book.

See [tree removal cost](/guides/tree-removal-cost-croydon/).


## What happens if you get it wrong

- An unlimited fine and up to six months in prison are possible for bird offences, according to council guidance.
- The person doing the work can be prosecuted as well as the owner.
- "I didn't check" is not a defence if a check was reasonable.
- Delay and extra cost if a job has to stop partway because a nest is found.

:::faq Bird nesting season and tree work: frequently asked questions
### Can I cut my hedge in nesting season?
There is no ban on garden hedges, but you must not damage an active nest. Check carefully and wait if you find one.

### When is bird nesting season in the UK?
The RSPB says March to September is the main season. The law sets no dates, and some birds start in January or February.

### Can I fell a tree in nesting season?
Yes, if a thorough check finds no active nests or bats. Otherwise it waits.

### What is the penalty for destroying a nest?
An unlimited fine and up to six months in prison are possible, according to council guidance.

### What counts as an active nest?
One being built or in use, with eggs or chicks. Protection ends when the young have left.

### I found a nest in my hedge. What now?
Stop, leave a buffer, don't move it, and wait until the young have fledged.

### Are bats protected in winter?
Yes. Bats and roosts are protected all year, even when bats aren't present.

### Does a TPO or conservation area permission cover wildlife?
No. Wildlife law is separate and still applies.

### Can you cut a leylandii hedge in summer?
Only if it can be checked. Dense conifers are the hardest, so autumn or winter is better, and never cut them back into bare brown wood.

### Does nesting season affect stump grinding?
Yes. Stumps can shelter birds, small animals and stag beetle larvae, so we check first.

### What if a tree is dangerous in nesting season?
Safety first. We limit work, check as far as is safe and record what we find.

### Who do I report a destroyed nest to?
The police on 101, asking for the Wildlife Crime Officer, or 999 if a crime is in progress.
