## Is your tree protected?

**No conservation area covers Sanderstead today.** Croydon has announced a review of nine places that could become conservation areas, and Sanderstead Village is one of them, described as an eighteenth-century village with a medieval parish church. The council said it will assess each area and then ask residents for their views, and it gave no dates. In practice:

- **For now,** the conservation area notice period does not apply to ordinary garden trees here.
- **If the village is designated,** it would apply to trees inside the boundary. We follow the council's consultations and flag anything that touches a customer's road.
- **A TPO is separate** and can protect a single mature tree anywhere.

**Green Belt land** such as the Plantation is about how land is used, not a rule on garden trees. Check TPOs separately.


## How we check for a TPO

Before we quote, we ask Croydon Council whether a Tree Preservation Order covers the tree, and we tell you the answer in writing. Croydon says consent is needed to prune or fell a protected tree, there is no application fee, and fines for unauthorised work can be heavy. A tree that is dead needs no formal permission, a dying tree needs consent, and a dangerous tree needs only the urgent work, with notice to the council. See [tree preservation orders](/guides/tree-preservation-orders-croydon/) and our guide to [permission to fell](/guides/do-i-need-permission-to-fell-a-tree/).


## Gardens beside Sanderstead Plantation and the Downs

Sanderstead Plantation, off Addington Road, is a council-owned wood of about 8.5 to 9 hectares on a chalk slope. It is a Site of Nature Conservation Importance within the Green Belt, noted for its bluebells in May and for breeding woodland birds. Records name beech, oak, ash, horse chestnut, sweet chestnut and sycamore among its trees. Beyond Sanderstead, the Sanderstead to Whyteleafe Countryside Area is part of the South London Downs National Nature Reserve, with chalk grassland, scrub and yew and broadleaved woodland on the edge of the built-up area.

If your garden borders either, four things come up every time:

- **The land behind the fence** is often the council's, so ask before you clear anything on it.
- **A big beech or oak of yours** that sheds a limb over the boundary is your liability, so have mature trees inspected, not just the ones that look unwell.
- **Overhang from the wood** can be trimmed to your side of the line and no further.
- **Ash.** It appears in the Plantation canopy, so if yours has a thinning crown see [ash dieback in Croydon](/guides/ash-dieback-croydon/).

Edges like this also hold nesting birds and bats, so spring and summer work gets an extra check. The [nesting season guide](/guides/bird-nesting-season-tree-work/) has the dates.


## Roots, chalk and your house

The RHS says subsidence is generally a problem only on shrinkable clay, and that gardens on chalk or sand are very unlikely to see tree-related damage. The Plantation sits on a chalk slope, which is a reason for less worry about big trees near the house in much of Sanderstead than in clay parts of Croydon. That is a general rule, not a guarantee for your plot, so check your ground before relying on it. Roots can still lift paving within about a metre of the trunk and can enter cracked drains. See [trees near buildings](/trees-near-buildings-croydon/) before you remove a large tree you think may be causing cracking.
