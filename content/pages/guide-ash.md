- **When must it go?** When it could fall on a house, road, path or play area. [Risk](#is-my-ash-dangerous).
- **Can I spot it myself?** Sometimes. Mid to late summer is easiest. [Signs](#signs-of-ash-dieback).
- **Do I need permission?** If it has a TPO, or you are in a conservation area, yes. [The rules](#the-rules-for-ash-trees).
- **What does it cost?** Often more than a healthy tree of the same size. [Cost](#what-ash-dieback-work-costs).


## What ash dieback is

Ash dieback is a fungal disease caused by *Hymenoscyphus fraxineus*, first confirmed in Britain in 2012. It spreads on the wind, which carries spores over local distances, and on the movement of infected ash plants. Spores are released from the previous year's fallen leaves between June and September. There is no cure, and it is usually fatal unless a tree is tolerant or resistant.

How fast it moves varies. Young trees are often killed quite quickly. Mature trees may survive for several years, and may eventually give way to secondary infections such as honey fungus. Some trees tolerate or resist the disease, and keeping healthy ash helps researchers find them.


## Signs of ash dieback

| Time of year | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Spring and summer** | Late or sparse leaf, blackened or wilting leaves that hang on, dead shoots at the ends of branches |
| **Mid to late summer** | The easiest time to spot it. Thin or patchy crown, premature leaf loss from the outer crown |
| **Autumn and winter** | Dead twigs, bare areas in the crown, new shoots growing straight from the trunk. Harder to tell, because trees are shedding leaves naturally |
| **Any time** | Diamond-shaped dark lesions on the bark, sunken or discoloured patches and bleeding sores where branches meet the stem |

**Caution:** many things look similar. Drought, root damage, ash canker, other fungi and old age can all thin a crown. **Rowan** (mountain ash) is often mistaken for ash but is not affected. A professional assessment is the only way to be sure. Photograph your tree in the same place every few weeks, because change over time is the strongest evidence.


## Is my ash dangerous?

Danger depends on how far the disease has progressed and what is within falling distance. A diseased ash in the middle of a field is a wildlife habitat. The same tree beside a house, road or path is a problem.

| Stage | What it looks like | What we usually advise |
|---|---|---|
| **Early** | Some thinning, no major dead limbs | Monitor, photograph, re-check in a season |
| **Moderate** | Dead branches, thinning crown, visible lesions | Prune out dead wood or reduce if there are targets, keep checking |
| **Advanced** | Large dead sections, collapsing crown, root or collar problems | Take down, in a planned way, before winter weather |
| **Dead** | No living growth | Remove promptly if it could fall on anything |

**Priority targets:** houses, conservatories, roads, play areas, rail lines, power lines and busy paths. Open ground is lower priority.

The Forestry Commission advises owners to check the root collar for signs of ash dieback or honey fungus, and to pay particular attention after storms and high winds, and near busy roads and public areas. It also says managing the risk is the owner's responsibility, and an owner may be liable if a tree or branch causes harm. See [storm-damaged trees](/guides/storm-damaged-tree-who-is-responsible/).


## What to do if you own an ash

1. **Don't panic and don't fell a healthy ash.** Councils and wildlife trusts advise leaving ash that is not a safety risk.
2. **Don't climb it, lean a ladder on it or work under it.** Infected wood can be brittle.
3. **Photograph it regularly,** and note what is within falling distance.
4. **Get a written assessment.** A [tree survey](/tree-surveys-croydon/) from £260 gives you a record for your insurer and neighbours.
5. **Decide:** monitor, prune or remove, based on the targets and the stage.
6. **Check the rules** below before any work.
7. **Minimise pruning of ash you keep.** Young regrowth is more susceptible.
8. **Use a qualified, insured tree surgeon,** and ask to see proof of both.

The Forestry Commission's Tree Alert service accepts reports of suspected cases, though at this stage of the outbreak you generally do not need to act unless it asks.


## Where ash is a problem in Croydon

Ash is recorded across the downland edge and woods:

- **Kenley Common:** the City of London says dieback has caused the loss of large stands and it has cleared severely affected ash along urban boundaries. See [Kenley](/tree-surgeon-kenley/).
- **Happy Valley and Farthing Downs:** ash is in the Happy Valley woodland and in the Farthing Downs tree belt. See [Coulsdon](/tree-surgeon-coulsdon/).
- **Selsdon Wood:** Croydon's plan describes ash-maple woodland on the slopes. See [Selsdon](/tree-surgeon-selsdon/).
- **Sanderstead Plantation and Croham Hurst:** ash is among the trees recorded. See [Sanderstead](/tree-surgeon-sanderstead/) and [South Croydon](/tree-surgeon-south-croydon/).

If your garden backs onto any of these, ask who owns the boundary strip, and look at the ash on both sides of the fence.


## The rules for ash trees

- **Felling licence.** Garden trees are generally exempt, as are orchards and fruit trees. A licence is mostly about woodland. If you own woodland, ask the Forestry Commission. See [do I need permission to fell a tree](/guides/do-i-need-permission-to-fell-a-tree/).
- **TPO.** Disease does not remove a TPO. Croydon says a dead tree needs no formal permission, a dying tree needs consent, and a dangerous tree allows only the urgent work needed, with five days' notice. See [tree preservation orders](/guides/tree-preservation-orders-croydon/).
- **Conservation area.** Six weeks' written notice for most work on a living tree. See [conservation area tree work](/guides/conservation-area-tree-work-croydon/).
- **Wildlife.** Ash has real wildlife value. Birds and bats use it, so non-urgent work belongs outside nesting season. See the [nesting season guide](/guides/bird-nesting-season-tree-work/).
- **Responsibility.** Owners for their own trees, the council for street and park trees, Network Rail for railway embankments, and landlords or managing agents for rented or communal land.


## Working on ash safely, and what to do with the wood

- **Brittle wood** is why we assess climbability first and use a platform, crane or rigging where needed, and control where each piece falls.
- **Fallen leaves** can carry the fungus, so collecting them helps slow spread. Wildlife trusts suggest burning where permitted, burying or composting them.
- **Firewood and timber.** The ban on moving ash applies to plants, seeds and trees, but not to ash timber or firewood, except from sites where the disease has been found. Ash logs can generally be kept.


## Replacing an ash

Avoid planting ash for now. Good alternatives include field maple, hornbeam, rowan, wild cherry, silver birch and hazel, planted well away from buildings. A mix of species is more resilient. If a protected tree is removed with consent, the council may require a replacement. Buy only from reputable suppliers with certified disease-free stock.

:::faq Ash dieback in Croydon: frequently asked questions
### Does my ash tree have to come down?
No. Councils advise against felling ash that is not a safety risk. Priority goes to trees that could fall on a house, road or path.

### How do I know if my ash has dieback?
Look for thin or sparse leaf, blackened leaves, dead shoots and diamond-shaped bark lesions. Mid to late summer is easiest. Get a professional assessment to be sure.

### Is ash dieback dangerous?
It can be. Infected wood becomes brittle, and trees near houses, roads and paths can drop limbs or fail.

### Can ash dieback be cured?
No. Some trees tolerate it, but there is no practical cure.

### Do I need permission to fell an ash with dieback?
If it has a TPO or is in a conservation area, yes. A dead tree needs no formal permission, a dying tree needs consent, and a dangerous tree allows only urgent work.

### Do I need a felling licence for a garden ash?
Generally no. Garden trees are exempt.

### Is a diseased tree still protected by a TPO?
Yes. Disease does not remove a TPO.

### Who is responsible if my ash falls?
You, as owner, if you knew or should have known it was dangerous. Take it seriously and keep records.

### Can I burn ash firewood?
Ash timber and firewood are generally fine to keep, except from sites where the disease has been found.

### What should I replace an ash with?
Field maple, hornbeam, rowan, wild cherry, silver birch or hazel, away from buildings.

### Is it cheaper to take down an ash early?
Often yes. Wood becomes more brittle as the disease advances, which makes the job slower and riskier.

### Can you check my ash for me?
Yes. Send photos or book a survey, and we tell you in writing how urgent it is.
